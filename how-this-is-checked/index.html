Why this is hard, specifically

Tennessee does not have one answer to “where is probate filed.” It has ninety-five.

Chancery court holds probate jurisdiction generally, under T.C.A. § 16-16-201, and that section grants it exclusive jurisdiction only “where not otherwise specifically provided by public, private, special or local acts.” That clause is the whole problem. Counties have their own private acts, some written in the 1940s and 1950s, many never amended when the general law moved. A separate statute, § 18-6-106, bars county clerks from clerking probate courts and then exempts a handful of counties by population bracket.

The result is a system with no single authoritative list. A county's private act can say one thing while the county's own courthouse does another, and both can be defensible. In at least one county the compiled act still names an office that the county's own website shows has nothing to do with estates.

So no honest site can promise perfection here. What it can do is say where each fact came from, keep checking, and tell you when it does not know.

Where the facts come from, in order of preference

  • The statute itself. For anything about what the law requires, the Tennessee Code and, where a law was recently rewritten, the enrolled text of the public act as passed. The 2023 small estate rewrite is read from the act, not from summaries of it.
  • The county's own published pages and forms. For which office, who runs it, what it costs, and what the local rules are.
  • State directories, last. They are authoritative and they lag. Where a state directory and a county's own site disagree, the county's site wins, because it is the one that updates itself.

Phone numbers have proven the most durable field and addresses the least, which is why the county directory leads with phone numbers and why every page tells you to call before you drive.

Commercially generated “probate in your county” pages are not used as sources at all. Several of them, carrying 2026 datestamps, still describe Tennessee's procedure as a small estate affidavit, which stopped being the mechanism in April 2023.

What gets checked, and how often

  • Weekly. The statutory core, because those are the claims that would make everything wrong at once. Plus a rotating slice of counties, so all ninety-five cycle through roughly every two months, with the highest-traffic counties re-checked every week.
  • Every page carries a dated stamp saying when it was last verified and against what.
  • The date only moves when a check actually ran. It is never advanced on a schedule. If a check cannot be completed, the old date stands. A freshness date nobody earned is worse than no date, and it is exactly what makes the stale pages elsewhere so convincing.
  • A record of each check is kept in the site's public repository, naming what was examined and what was found, so the claim is something you can audit rather than something you have to take on faith.

Independent review

Work here is checked by reviewers who are given the published pages and the primary sources, and deliberately not given the reasoning behind them. They are told that finding nothing is a worse outcome than finding something.

That design is not decorative. Re-reading your own work finds typographical errors and misses errors of premise, because catching those requires disbelieving the person who wrote it. Every substantial error listed below was found that way, and none of them was found by re-reading.

What has been found wrong, and fixed

Listing this is the point. A site that claims nothing has ever been wrong with it is telling you how carefully it has been checked, which is nothing.

  • A county page named the wrong office entirely. Rutherford County probate goes to the County Clerk, not the Clerk and Master. Three official sources had been consulted, carefully, about an office that had nothing to do with probate.
  • Five misstatements of law in the first week, including bond described as discretionary when the statute makes it mandatory, and creditor claims not excluded from the small estate track when the statute bars them outright.
  • Jefferson County pointed at the Chancery line. Probate there runs through the County Clerk, with its own intake form and fee schedule.
  • Loudon and Rhea both pointed at offices that do not take estate filings. Rhea's listed office was stripped of probate authority by a 1982 act.
  • Van Buren cited a court that does not exist in that county. That act belonged to a different county.
  • A phone number was published for Rutherford that appears nowhere on the county's own site, taken on an auditor's word. It traced back to exactly the commercially generated pages this site tells you not to trust.
  • Three further misstatements of law, found by reading the 2023 act in full: no statute directs a clerk to assist you, though most do help; the bond exemption is plural, so petitioners who are together the only heirs are exempt; and muniment of title covers personal property as well as real estate.

Every one of those is corrected. They are listed because the pattern matters more than the individual errors: the dangerous ones were never the facts that looked uncertain. They were the facts that looked settled.

What is still unknown

Two counties are marked Unsettled in state law in the directory rather than given an answer.

In Scott County, a 1982 private act vests probate jurisdiction in General Sessions and names no clerk, a 1951 act makes the Circuit Court Clerk the clerk of General Sessions, and the general statute points instead at the Clerk and Master. The county publishes nothing either way. Both offices are legally defensible.

In Lauderdale County, a 2025 private act dissolves the Probate and Juvenile Court when the sitting judge's term expires, and separately allows the county to move the clerking at any point before that. No published record says whether it has. Three buildings are in play.

Both entries give you the numbers and the question to ask. Guessing would have been easy and would have read as authoritative, which is the problem.

What this is not

This is organization and routing, not legal advice. The difference is concrete rather than a formality.

What a statute requires is the same for everybody, and this site can tell you that. Whether your mother's brokerage account was titled in a way that counts toward the fifty thousand dollar ceiling depends on a document nobody here has seen, and getting it wrong has consequences that land on you personally.

For a contested will, real estate with a clouded title, an estate that cannot pay its debts, or a family structure with any complication in it, talk to a probate attorney. An hour of advice on one specific question is the cheapest thing in this entire process and almost nobody buys it.
