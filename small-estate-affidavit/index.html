Tennessee probate · Small estates

Tennessee does not have a small estate affidavit anymore

It was repealed in 2023. If you came here holding a form you downloaded, or planning to walk one into a bank, that is the thing to know before you spend another hour on it.

Last verified October 27, 2026 Every county's filing office re-checked against the county's own published pages. Statutory claims checked against the Tennessee Code.

What replaced it

Public Chapter 297, effective April 28, 2023, rewrote the Small Estate Probate Act. The affidavit you presented directly to a bank is gone. In its place is a sworn petition filed with the court clerk, who then issues Limited Letters. You take the Limited Letters to the bank instead.

The old way and the new way

The affidavit the forms describe was itself filed with the court clerk, indexed, and returned to you stamped and certified. That was already the law under the Small Estate Affidavit Limited Letter of Authority Act before 2023. Guides that describe walking an affidavit straight into a bank are describing something older than that, and they were wrong even then.

Under the current statute you file a petition with the clerk in the county where the person lived. The petition lists every asset with its value and every known debt with the creditor's name, address, and amount. The court can require a bond, payable to the clerk, and it can require one even when the will says bond is waived. The clerk then issues Limited Letters, and those are what the bank accepts.

What Public Chapter 297 actually changed in 2023: the affidavit became a petition, the letters were renamed and given a statutory form, the bond rules were rewritten, creditors lost the ability to start a small estate, and creditor notice and claims were barred from the track entirely. The courthouse trip and the filing fee were already there. It is still far shorter than a full administration.

Whether you qualify

  • $50,000 or less in personal property that was in the person's name alone. That is the whole test, and it has been $50,000 since 2014.
  • Real estate does not count toward it, and cannot pass through it. Not the house, not land, not a share of family property, whatever the value. The statute counts personal property only. A small estate petition is never valid to transfer real estate, and every county form says so in capital letters.
  • 45 days since the date of death before the petition can be filed, though the court may waive that wait for good cause. The track is also closed if anyone has already filed to appoint a personal representative.
  • Anything with a named beneficiary, anything held jointly with right of survivorship, and anything in a trust is left out of the count entirely. Those pass on their own and were never part of probate.

If additional assets turn up later that push the total past $50,000, the matter does not convert on its own. Somebody has to file a verified petition asking the court to convert it, and the statute puts personal liability on the representative and on anyone already paid out. Counting carefully at the start is worth an afternoon.

Why the wrong version is still everywhere

Probate is a subject with enormous search traffic and no one checking the answers. Pages get generated, they describe a process that was accurate before April 2023, and nothing in the machinery that ranks them cares that the statute moved. Several sites currently near the top for Tennessee small estate questions still tell readers to take an affidavit to a bank.

Two names on the same page are a tell. Some counties still title the line item on their fee schedule "Affidavit for Small Estate" because the schedule was never renamed, while the form underneath it is a petition for Limited Letters. The fee schedule is old wording. The form is the law.

The way to be sure, always, is the clerk's office in the county where the person lived. They have the current form, and the statute tells them to help you fill it in.

What the petition asks you for

  • Every asset in the person's name alone, with a date-of-death value. Banks will give you the balance as of that date if you bring a death certificate.
  • Every known debt, with the creditor's name, mailing address, and the amount. You list them; they cannot file claims against you. No creditor notice is published in a small estate and no creditor may file a claim in one.
  • The original will, if there is one. A copy will not do, and Limited Letters will not issue until the original is filed.
  • Written consents from the other adult heirs where they agree you should serve.
  • Your own identification, and payment the clerk's office accepts. Some take no personal checks, some no money orders, and Rutherford County refuses any check not drawn on a Tennessee bank.
  • A corporate surety bond equal to the value of the property, unless you are excused. The statute requires it whatever the will says. Three things excuse it: you are the sole heir of someone who died without a will, you are the sole beneficiary under the will, or every adult heir and beneficiary consents in writing. Get those consents signed before you go.

With a will, what issues is Limited Letters Testamentary. Without one, Limited Letters of Administration. The petition is slightly different; the asset and debt lists are the same work either way.

Where you file it

In the county where the person lived, and the office differs by county more than most guides admit.
